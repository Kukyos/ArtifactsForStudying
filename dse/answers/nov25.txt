<p>Null (missing) values must be handled before modelling because they bias the mean and correlations and most algorithms cannot take NaN. The techniques are:</p>
<ol>
<li><b>Delete</b> rows or columns: <code>df.dropna()</code> (any row with a null), <code>df.dropna(axis=1)</code> (columns with a null), <code>df.dropna(how='any', subset=['marks'])</code> (only rows null in chosen columns). Simple, but loses data; use only when few values are missing.</li>

=== Nov25 Q22b
<h4>Linear regression</h4>
<p>Linear regression is a supervised learning technique that models the relationship between a dependent variable y (continuous) and an independent variable x by a straight line. Simple linear regression: <b>y = b0 + b1 x</b>, where b0 is the intercept, b1 the slope (regression coefficient). The error (residual) of a point is e = y - y_hat (actual - predicted). The best-fit line is the one with the smallest sum of squared errors.</p>
<h4>Least-squares derivation</h4>
```gbox
S = sum (yi - b0 - b1 xi)^2                      (sum of squared errors, to be minimised)

dS/db0 = -2 sum (yi - b0 - b1 xi) = 0      =>   sum y  = n b0 + b1 sum x          ...(1)
dS/db1 = -2 sum xi (yi - b0 - b1 xi) = 0   =>   sum xy = b0 sum x + b1 sum x^2    ...(2)

From (1):   b0 = ybar - b1 xbar
Put in (2): b1 = [ n sum xy - sum x sum y ] / [ n sum x^2 - (sum x)^2 ]
               = sum (x - xbar)(y - ybar) / sum (x - xbar)^2
```
<p>The best-fit line is therefore <b>y = b0 + b1 x</b> with b1 = Sxy / Sxx and b0 = ybar - b1 xbar.</p>
<p>Check with numbers: for x = 1 to 5, y = 2, 4, 5, 4, 5: xbar = 3, ybar = 4, Sxy = 6, Sxx = 10, so b1 = 0.6, b0 = 4 - 0.6 x 3 = 2.2 and y = 2.2 + 0.6x (see Apr26 Q19a).</p>

=== Nov25 Q23a
<table class="work"><tr><th></th><th>User-based CF</th><th>Item-based CF</th></tr>
<tr><td>Idea</td><td>"users like you liked this": find users whose ratings are similar to the target user's and recommend what they liked</td><td>"people who liked this item also liked that": find items whose rating patterns are similar to items the user liked</td></tr>
<tr><td>Similarity computed between</td><td>users (rows of the utility matrix)</td><td>items (columns of the utility matrix)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Prediction</td><td>weighted average of the similar users' ratings of the item</td><td>weighted average of the target user's own ratings of similar items</td></tr>
<tr><td>Similarity measure</td><td colspan="2">cosine or Pearson (centred cosine) similarity</td></tr>
<tr><td>Advantages</td><td>captures changing tastes of the community; surprising, diverse recommendations; easy to explain</td><td>item similarities are stable, so they can be computed offline and reused; scales better when users outnumber items; works with few ratings per user</td></tr>
<tr><td>Disadvantages</td><td>users change and ratings are sparse, so similarity is unstable; recomputing for millions of users is slow; cold start for new users</td><td>needs enough ratings per item; cold start for new items; recommendations tend to be obvious and less diverse</td></tr></table>
<p>Steps common to both: build the utility matrix, compute similarity (cosine), choose the K most similar users/items, predict the missing rating, recommend the highest predictions. Both need many ratings and suffer from sparsity and the cold-start problem.</p>

=== Nov25 Q23b
<p>Performance of a recommender is measured in three ways.</p>
<ol>
<li><b>Prediction error</b> (predicted vs actual rating): <b>MAE</b> = (1/n) sum |actual - predicted|; <b>RMSE</b> = sqrt((1/n) sum (actual - predicted)^2). Lower is better; RMSE punishes large errors more.</li>
<li><b>Top-N quality:</b> <b>Hit rate</b> = hits / users, where a hit is a recommended item the user really liked or rated; <b>ARHR</b> (average reciprocal hit rank) = average of 1/rank of the hits, so a hit at the top counts more. Precision and recall of the top-N list are also used.</li>
<li><b>Beyond accuracy:</b> <b>coverage</b> (share of items the system can recommend), <b>diversity</b> = 1 - average similarity of the recommended items, <b>novelty</b> (how unpopular the recommended items are).</li>
</ol>

<h4>Analysis of the ethical implications</h4>
<table class="work"><tr><th>Issue</th><th>What went wrong in the fitness app</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Privacy</b></td><td>Location, heart rate and sleep data are highly personal (health and movement patterns). Users lost control over who sees it; a breach exposed it, and linking it with public data re-identified people (a <i>linkage attack</i>), so "anonymised" was not really anonymous. Possible harms: profiling, stalking, discrimination by insurers or employers.</td></tr>

<tr><td><b>Informed consent</b></td><td>Users were not clearly told that their data would go to advertisers, and had no easy opt-out. Consent must be specific, informed and freely given; sharing for a new purpose (advertising) without it breaks purpose limitation.</td></tr></table>
<h4>Ethical measures I would implement</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Clear opt-in consent</b> in plain language for each use, separate consent for sharing with third parties, an easy opt-out and the right to delete data.</li>
<li><b>Purpose limitation and data minimisation</b>: collect only what the health insights need; do not sell or share beyond the stated purpose.</li>
<li><b>Transparency and accountability</b>: a readable privacy policy, a named data-protection owner, an audit trail, and prompt breach notification to users and the regulator (GDPR/HIPAA-style compliance).</li>
<li><b>Contracts and audits</b> with advertisers: no re-identification, no onward sharing; regular privacy audits.</li>
</ul>
<h4>Technical measures</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Proper anonymisation</b>: remove direct identifiers (suppression), generalise (exact location to city or area, age to age range), and test for re-identification with <b>k-anonymity</b>.</li>
<li><b>Differential privacy</b>: add calibrated noise to aggregates released to advertisers so no individual can be singled out.</li>
<li><b>Encryption</b> of data in storage and in transit, strong <b>access control</b> (role-based, least privilege), pseudonymised IDs kept separate from the data.</li>
<li><b>Share aggregated or synthetic data</b>, not individual-level records; keep raw data on the device or on-premises where possible.</li>
<li><b>Retention limit</b>: delete data when it is no longer needed; monitor and log access to detect a breach early.</li>
</ul>
<p>Together these follow the 5 C's of data ethics: <b>consent, confidentiality, control, compliance and communication</b>.</p>
