=== ETH Q1
<h4>(a) Ethical concerns: smart-city healthcare analytics</h4>
<table class="work"><tr><th>Concern</th><th>Why it matters here</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Privacy of sensitive data</b></td><td>Medical histories and treatment records are among the most sensitive personal data. Combining records from many hospitals builds a detailed profile of each patient, and exposure can cause discrimination, stigma, or loss of insurance or jobs.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Informed consent and purpose</b></td><td>Patients gave their data for treatment, not for a government analytics system. Using it for trend prediction without clear consent breaks purpose limitation.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Confidentiality and security</b></td><td>A central database of many hospitals' records is a high-value target for breaches and for misuse by insiders.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Re-identification</b></td><td>Removing names is not enough: age, postcode, rare disease and dates can be linked with other data to identify a person.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Bias and fairness</b></td><td>Records from some hospitals or groups may be missing or unrepresentative, so predictions can be worse for under-represented communities and misdirect public health resources.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Transparency and accountability</b></td><td>Citizens should know what is collected and who answers if the system errs or is misused; the department that built it must be accountable.</td></tr></table>
<h4>(b) Guidelines and technical measures</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Ethical and legal guidelines:</b> follow HIPAA/GDPR-style rules; collect only the data needed (data minimisation); use it only for the stated public-health purpose; obtain clear consent or a lawful basis and give an opt-out; set a retention limit; form an ethics board to approve uses; publish a plain-language policy.</li>
<li><b>De-identification and anonymisation:</b> remove direct identifiers (suppression); generalise ages to ranges and postcodes to districts; test with k-anonymity; replace IDs with pseudonyms kept apart from the data.</li>
<li><b>Differential privacy:</b> add calibrated noise to published statistics so no individual affects the result noticeably.</li>
<li><b>Security:</b> encryption at rest and in transit, role-based access control with least privilege, audit logs, secure data-sharing agreements.</li>
<li><b>Privacy-preserving analytics:</b> keep data at each hospital and share only model updates (federated learning) or aggregates.</li>
<li><b>Fairness and accountability:</b> check data coverage and model error across groups, audit regularly, keep a human in the loop for decisions, name a responsible owner, and give people a way to complain and to have their data corrected.</li>
</ul>

=== ETH Q2
<h4>(a) Ethical concerns: online shopping analytics</h4>
<table class="work"><tr><th>Concern</th><th>In this scenario</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Privacy</b></td><td>Purchase history, browsing and preferences reveal habits, health, finances and beliefs. Customers did not expect them to leave the company.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Confidentiality</b></td><td>Data given to the shop was passed to advertising partners without authorisation; the company lost control of where it went.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Informed consent</b></td><td>Customers were not told about the sharing. Consent hidden in long terms is not informed consent; the data was used for a new purpose (advertising) they never agreed to.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Re-identification</b></td><td>The "anonymised" data was joined with public data and customers were identified (a linkage attack), so the anonymisation was inadequate and the promise of anonymity was false.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Trust and harm</b></td><td>Profiling can lead to manipulation, price discrimination and unwanted targeting; a breach of trust damages the company.</td></tr></table>
<h4>(b) Ethical practices and technical safeguards</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Practices:</b> tell customers clearly what is collected, why and with whom it is shared; opt-in consent for sharing with partners and an easy opt-out; purpose limitation and data minimisation; contracts that forbid partners from re-identifying or passing on the data; privacy audits and a data-protection officer; breach notification; compliance with GDPR-type law.</li>
<li><b>Anonymisation done properly:</b> remove or generalise quasi-identifiers (exact age to range, postcode to region, exact time to date) and check k-anonymity before release.</li>
<li><b>Differential privacy or noise addition</b> on anything shared; share aggregates or synthetic data rather than raw individual records.</li>
<li><b>Security:</b> encryption, access control, logging, and separating identifiers from behaviour data.</li>
<li><b>Re-identification testing:</b> try to link the release with public data before sharing it (a red-team test).</li>
</ul>

=== ETH Q3
<h4>(a) Ethical concerns: employee monitoring</h4>
<table class="work"><tr><th>Concern</th><th>In this scenario</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Privacy</b></td><td>Monitoring emails, computer use and location, possibly outside work hours or in private spaces, intrudes on employees' private life.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Confidentiality</b></td><td>Emails and activity logs contain personal and sensitive information; access by managers or leaks could expose it, and it could be used beyond appraisal.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Transparency</b></td><td>Employees are not told what is collected or how it is used, so they cannot question or correct it.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Informed consent</b></td><td>Consent given by signing an employment contract is not free and informed, because refusing may mean losing the job (power imbalance).</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Fairness</b></td><td>Counting keystrokes, hours online or emails ignores quality of work and can wrongly penalise some people (carers, part-timers, creative roles); it can create stress and distrust.</td></tr></table>
<h4>(b) Guidelines and technical measures</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Guidelines:</b> a written monitoring policy shared with all staff before it starts: what is collected, why, who sees it, how long it is kept; get acknowledgement or consent; collect only data relevant to performance (data minimisation); never monitor private communication or off-duty location; give employees access to their data and a way to challenge results; human review of decisions; ethics or HR committee oversight; follow labour and data-protection law.</li>
<li><b>Technical measures:</b> encryption and secure storage; role-based access (only HR and the direct manager, with logging); aggregate or pseudonymise the data for team-level analysis; automatic deletion after the retention period; use outcome-based measures (tasks completed, quality) rather than constant surveillance; test the scoring model for bias across groups and document how it works (explainability).</li>
</ul>

=== ETH Q4
<h4>(a) Ethical issues: social media analysis</h4>
<table class="work"><tr><th>Issue</th><th>In this scenario</th></tr>
<tr><td><b>Privacy</b></td><td>Posts, comments, likes and shares expose interests, opinions, relationships, politics and health. Even public posts were not shared for research profiling.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Informed consent</b></td><td>Users did not agree to be studied, and "it was public" is not consent to profiling and prediction of their behaviour.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Confidentiality</b></td><td>The organisation holds large amounts of personal content and must protect it from leaks and misuse.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Re-identification</b></td><td>Removing usernames does not anonymise: writing style, locations, timestamps and connections, joined with public profiles, identify the individuals. This is a linkage attack.</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Misuse</b></td><td>Predictions about interests and behaviour can be used for manipulation, targeted political messaging or discrimination.</td></tr></table>
<h4>(b) Privacy-preserving techniques and ethical practices</h4>
<ul>
<li><b>Techniques:</b> k-anonymity by generalising or suppressing quasi-identifiers (exact location, timestamps, rare attributes); noise addition; <b>differential privacy</b> for released statistics and models; pseudonymisation with the key stored separately; analysing aggregates rather than individual users; federated learning or secure storage with encryption and access control; testing a release against public data for re-identification before sharing.</li>
<li><b>Practices:</b> obtain ethics-board (IRB) approval; seek informed consent or, where impossible, use only data the platform terms allow and tell users how it is used; data minimisation and purpose limitation; a retention limit; do not publish or share raw data; accountability for who may access it; transparent reporting of methods and limitations.</li>
</ul>
