=== 1.1
<h2>From the universe to the need for quantum computing</h2>
<p>This is the opening thread of your Unit 1 slides (and the Mind-Map and Reflection assignments):</p>
```gbox
Universe → matter → molecules → atoms → electrons, protons, neutrons (fundamental particles)
   │ everyday objects obey CLASSICAL PHYSICS (Newton's laws, continuous values)
   ▼
Transistor = electronic switch → Moore's law: transistors per chip double ~every 2 years
   │ smaller transistors → more power … until they reach ATOMIC size
   ▼
At that scale QUANTUM effects take over (tunnelling, discreteness) → classical chips become unreliable
   ▼
Use quantum mechanics itself to compute → QUANTUM COMPUTING
```
<h2>Why classical computing hits a wall</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Physical limit.</b> Transistor gates are only a few nanometres (a few dozen atoms) across. Electrons <b>tunnel</b> through barriers that should block them, so leakage current, heat and errors rise, and Moore's law slows down.</li>
<li><b>Exponential complexity.</b> Simulating n interacting quantum particles needs about 2ⁿ numbers. 50 qubits is already 2⁵⁰ ≈ 10¹⁵ amplitudes, and caffeine-sized molecules are beyond any supercomputer. Feynman (1982): "Nature isn't classical… if you want to simulate it, you'd better make it quantum mechanical."</li>
</ul>
<h2>The quantum ideas that make the difference</h2>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>Classical worldview</th><th>Quantum worldview</th></tr>
<tr><td>Values are continuous</td><td>Energy and momentum come in <b>discrete packets</b> (quanta)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Particle or wave</td><td><b>Wave–particle duality</b>: de Broglie λ = h/p. Electrons diffract and interfere.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Things have definite properties</td><td><b>Superposition</b>: a definite outcome appears only on measurement</td></tr>
<tr><td>Measure with any precision</td><td><b>Heisenberg uncertainty</b>: Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2, built into nature</td></tr>
<tr><td>Deterministic</td><td><b>Probabilistic</b> outcomes (Born rule)</td></tr></table>
<p><b>Double-slit experiment:</b> unobserved particles build up an interference pattern, as waves would. Watching which slit they pass through destroys the pattern. Interference of amplitudes, and its destruction by measurement, is exactly what quantum algorithms exploit.</p>
<h2>Bit vs qubit</h2>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>Classical bit</th><th>Qubit</th></tr>
<tr><td>0 or 1</td><td>α|0⟩ + β|1⟩: both, with complex amplitudes</td></tr>
<tr><td>n bits hold 1 of 2ⁿ values</td><td>n qubits hold amplitudes for all 2ⁿ values</td></tr>
<tr><td>Can be read and copied freely</td><td>Reading collapses it; it cannot be copied (no-cloning)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Independent bits</td><td>Can be <b>entangled</b></td></tr></table>
<h2>Applications (from the slides)</h2>
<ul>
<li><b>Molecular simulation:</b> drug discovery, protein folding (Alzheimer's), materials, personalised medicine.</li>
<li><b>Security, a threat and a shield:</b> Shor's algorithm breaks RSA and ECC, while QKD (BB84) gives eavesdrop-evident key exchange.</li>
<li><b>Optimisation:</b> travelling salesperson and logistics, portfolio optimisation, smart-city traffic (annealing, QAOA).</li>
<li><b>Search and machine learning:</b> Grover (√N), quantum kernels, Boltzmann sampling.</li>
</ul>
<p class="callout">Exam tip: a 150–200-word "why do we need quantum computing" answer should cover (1) matter is quantum, (2) classical physics and Moore's law, (3) the atomic-scale limit through tunnelling and heat, (4) exponential simulation cost, (5) quantum computing uses superposition, entanglement and interference.</p>

<h2>System, state, wave-function, probability</h2>
<p>A <b>quantum system</b> is the object (electron, photon, atom, ion, qubit). Its <b>state</b> |ψ⟩ contains everything that can be known about it. Written in a basis, the state becomes a <b>wave-function</b> ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩, and |ψ(x)|² is the <b>probability density</b>.</p>
<h2>The five postulates</h2>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>#</th><th>Postulate</th><th>Formula</th><th>For a qubit</th></tr>
<tr><td>1</td><td><b>State</b>: a unit vector in a complex Hilbert space</td><td>|ψ⟩ ∈ H, ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1</td><td>α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, |α|² + |β|² = 1</td></tr>
<tr><td>2</td><td><b>Observables</b> are Hermitian operators. Results are eigenvalues.</td><td>A = A† = Σ aₖ|k⟩⟨k|</td><td>Z has eigenvalues ±1</td></tr>
<tr><td>3</td><td><b>Measurement</b>: Born rule + collapse</td><td>P(aₖ) = |⟨k|ψ⟩|², then |ψ⟩ → |k⟩</td><td>P(0) = |α|²</td></tr>
<tr><td>4</td><td><b>Evolution</b> is unitary (Schrödinger)</td><td>iħ d|ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|ψ⟩, U = e^{−iĤt/ħ}</td><td>gates X, H, CNOT…</td></tr>
<tr><td>5</td><td><b>Composite systems</b>: tensor product</td><td>H_AB = H_A ⊗ H_B</td><td>n qubits are 2ⁿ-dimensional</td></tr></table>
<h2>Hilbert space</h2>
<p>A complex vector space with an inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩. You need vectors for superposition, complex numbers for phase and interference, and the inner product for probabilities and orthogonality.</p>
<h2>Expectation value</h2>
```gbox
⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ = Σ aₖ P(aₖ)            e.g. |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩ :  ⟨Z⟩ = 9/25 − 16/25 = −7/25
```
<h2>Measurement and collapse</h2>
<ul><li>Measurement is random, with outcomes weighted by |amplitude|².</li>
<li>Afterwards the state <b>is</b> the eigenstate that was observed. Measuring again gives the same answer.</li>
<li>It is not unitary and not reversible: the phase information is gone.</li></ul>
<p class="callout">Remember the pairing: <b>unitary evolution between measurements, collapse at a measurement</b>. Every circuit question is a chain of unitaries followed by one measurement.</p>

<h2>The qubit</h2>
```gbox
|0⟩ = [1]   |1⟩ = [0]       |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = [α]      α, β ∈ ℂ ,  |α|² + |β|² = 1
      [0]         [1]                            [β]
```
<p>α and β are <b>probability amplitudes</b>. Measuring in the computational (Z) basis gives 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|² (the <b>Born rule</b>). The state then collapses to the observed basis state.</p>
<h2>Normalisation recipe</h2>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Add the squared magnitudes. For complex amplitudes, |a + ib|² = a² + b².</li>
<li>Divide the whole state by the square root of that sum.</li>
<li>Square each amplitude to get the probabilities, and check that they add to 1.</li>
</ol>
<p>Example: 2|0⟩ + (1 − i)|1⟩ gives 4 + 2 = 6, so (1/√6)(2|0⟩ + (1 − i)|1⟩), with P(0) = 2/3 and P(1) = 1/3.</p>
<h2>Useful states</h2>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>State</th><th>Vector</th><th>Z-basis probabilities</th></tr>
<tr><td>|+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2</td><td>(1, 1)/√2</td><td>½, ½</td></tr>
<tr><td>|−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2</td><td>(1, −1)/√2</td><td>½, ½</td></tr>
<tr><td>|+i⟩ = (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2</td><td>(1, i)/√2</td><td>½, ½</td></tr></table>
<p>|+⟩ and |−⟩ give the same Z statistics but are different states: they differ by a <b>relative phase</b>. Only a <b>global</b> phase (e^{iγ}|ψ⟩) is physically meaningless.</p>
<h2>Several qubits</h2>
<p>n qubits have 2ⁿ amplitudes, one for each bit string. P(string) = |amplitude|². P(one qubit = 0) is the sum over all strings with that bit 0. Example: (|00⟩ + 2|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√6 gives P(01) = 4/6, and P(first qubit 0) = (1 + 4)/6 = 5/6.</p>
<p class="callout">Always check normalisation first. IA2 Q3 prints 1/√10 for a state whose squares add to 14: the correct factor is 1/√14.</p>

<h2>Bloch sphere</h2>
```gbox
|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩        (x, y, z) = (sinθ cosφ , sinθ sinφ , cosθ)
P(0) = cos²(θ/2)    P(1) = sin²(θ/2)
```
<ul><li><b>θ</b> is the angle from the north pole. It controls the probabilities.</li>
<li><b>φ</b> is the angle around the z axis. It is the relative phase.</li>
<li>Pure states lie <b>on</b> the sphere (radius 1). Mixed states lie <b>inside</b> it (topic 1.5).</li></ul>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>State</th><th>θ</th><th>φ</th><th>Point</th></tr>
<tr><td>|0⟩</td><td>0</td><td>–</td><td>(0, 0, 1) north pole</td></tr>
<tr><td>|1⟩</td><td>π</td><td>–</td><td>(0, 0, −1) south pole</td></tr>
<tr><td>|+⟩</td><td>π/2</td><td>0</td><td>(1, 0, 0)</td></tr>
<tr><td>|−⟩</td><td>π/2</td><td>π</td><td>(−1, 0, 0)</td></tr>
<tr><td>|±i⟩</td><td>π/2</td><td>±π/2</td><td>(0, ±1, 0)</td></tr></table>
<p><b>Worked example</b> (Practice Q13): cos(π/6)|0⟩ + e^{iπ/3} sin(π/6)|1⟩ gives θ = π/3 and φ = π/3, so (x, y, z) = (√3/4, 3/4, 1/2), P(0) = 3/4, and the point is in the upper hemisphere (z &gt; 0).</p>
<p>Gates are <b>rotations</b>: X is 180° about x, Y is 180° about y, Z is 180° about z, S is 90° about z, T is 45° about z, and H is 180° about (x + z)/√2.</p>
<h2>Spin-½ as a qubit</h2>
```gbox
|↑⟩ = |0⟩, |↓⟩ = |1⟩        S_z = (ħ/2)σ_z = (ħ/2)[1  0]      eigenvalues ±ħ/2
                                               [0 −1]
P(+ħ/2) = |α|² ,  P(−ħ/2) = |β|² ,  ⟨S_z⟩ = (ħ/2)(|α|² − |β|²)
```
<p>Example: (3/5)|↑⟩ + (4i/5)|↓⟩ gives P = 9/25 and 16/25, so ⟨S_z⟩ = −7ħ/50. After the measurement the spin <b>is</b> |↑⟩ or |↓⟩ (collapse). Measuring S_x afterwards would be 50/50 again, because S_x and S_z do not commute.</p>

<h2>Density matrix</h2>
```gbox
pure:   ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|            mixed:  ρ = Σ pᵢ |ψᵢ⟩⟨ψᵢ|
Tr ρ = 1      ρ = ρ†      ρ ≥ 0       P(m) = Tr(P_m ρ) = ⟨m|ρ|m⟩      ⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA)
pure  ⇔ Tr ρ² = 1          mixed ⇔ Tr ρ² < 1   (minimum 1/2 for a qubit, at ρ = I/2)
```
<p><b>Recipe for |ψ⟩⟨ψ|:</b> write the column vector, multiply by its conjugate transpose (row), and fill the matrix entry by entry.</p>
```gbox
|ψ⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2  →  ρ = ½[1][1  −i] = ½[1  −i]      Tr ρ² = 1 (pure), ⟨Z⟩ = 0
                               [i]          [i   1]
0.6|0⟩⟨0| + 0.4|+⟩⟨+|  →  ρ = [0.8 0.2]   Tr ρ² = 0.76 (mixed),  P(0) = 0.8
                              [0.2 0.2]
```
<p><b>Diagonal entries</b> are the probabilities. <b>Off-diagonal entries</b> are the coherences (phase information). A 50/50 mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩ (I/2) and the pure state |+⟩ have the same diagonal, but only |+⟩ has the off-diagonal ½ that lets H turn it back into |0⟩.</p>
<p>Bloch form: ρ = ½(I + xX + yY + zZ), where |r| = 1 for pure states and |r| &lt; 1 for mixed. Half of a Bell pair has ρ = I/2, the centre of the ball.</p>

<h2>Toolkit</h2>
<table class="tbl"><tr><th>Tool</th><th>Statement</th><th>Quantum meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>Inner product</td><td>⟨a|b⟩ = Σ aᵢ* bᵢ</td><td>overlap; |⟨a|b⟩|² is a probability</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cauchy–Schwarz</td><td>|⟨a|b⟩|² ≤ ⟨a|a⟩⟨b|b⟩</td><td>probabilities ≤ 1; leads to the uncertainty principle</td></tr>
<tr><td>Gram–Schmidt</td><td>uₖ = vₖ − Σ⟨eⱼ|vₖ⟩eⱼ, normalise</td><td>build orthonormal measurement bases. (1,1), (1,0) → |+⟩, |−⟩</td></tr>
<tr><td>Outer product</td><td>|a⟩⟨b|; P = |v⟩⟨v|</td><td>projectors, density matrices</td></tr>
<tr><td>Spectral decomposition</td><td>A = Σ λₖ|vₖ⟩⟨vₖ|</td><td>an observable is its eigenvalues + projectors. X = |+⟩⟨+| − |−⟩⟨−|</td></tr>
<tr><td>Hermitian</td><td>A = A†</td><td>real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, so a valid observable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Unitary</td><td>U†U = I</td><td>gates and time evolution; preserves inner products</td></tr>
<tr><td>Pauli basis</td><td>A = a₀I + a₁X + a₂Y + a₃Z, aₖ = ½Tr(σₖA)</td><td>any qubit observable; eigenvalues a₀ ± |a⃗|</td></tr>
<tr><td>Commutator</td><td>[A, B] = AB − BA; [X, Y] = 2iZ</td><td>ΔAΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩|</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tensor product</td><td>(A⊗B)(|a⟩⊗|b⟩) = A|a⟩⊗B|b⟩</td><td>composite systems</td></tr>
<tr><td>SVD / polar</td><td>A = UΣV† = U√(A†A)</td><td>Schmidt decomposition, so an entanglement test; POVM elements</td></tr>
<tr><td>POVM</td><td>Eₘ ≥ 0, ΣEₘ = I</td><td>generalized measurement; unambiguous discrimination</td></tr></table>
<h2>Eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix, fast</h2>
```gbox
λ² − (Tr A)λ + det A = 0       e.g.  [1  2i]: Tr 2, det 1 − 4 = −3  →  λ² − 2λ − 3 = 0  →  λ = 3, −1
                                     [−2i 1]
```
